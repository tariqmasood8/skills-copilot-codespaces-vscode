Subject matter

What international finance studies

International finance studies how real and financial claims cross national borders — how they are priced, financed, recorded, and governed — when the parties use different currencies and answer to different sovereigns.

The field has two halves that meet constantly:

Micro

International financial management

Currency markets, hedging, cross-border investment and funding decisions of firms and banks.

Macro

Open-economy macroeconomics

Balance of payments, exchange rates, capital flows, reserves, crises and policy regimes.

This paper leans macro — but the two are inseparable in practice.

Four questions the field answers
  • What is the price of a currency, and what moves it?
  • How does a country keep score with the rest of the world — and can that position be sustained?
  • Why does capital flow where it does, and why does it sometimes stop suddenly?
  • Who writes the rules, holds the reserves, and lends when everyone else refuses?

Each question maps to one unit of this course, in that order.

Scope

Four things domestic finance never has to deal with

A rupee loan in Lucknow and a dollar loan to Lucknow are not the same contract. The differences are what this course is about.

01 — Currency

More than one unit of account

Every cross-border claim carries an exchange rate. A profitable investment can still lose money on the currency alone — so pricing, hedging and parity conditions become unavoidable.

02 — Sovereignty

Countries, not just counterparties

There is no global bankruptcy court and no global lender of last resort. Country risk, expropriation, sanctions and default without foreclosure are permanent features.

03 — Segmentation

Markets that do not fully join up

Taxes, disclosure rules, capital controls and information gaps keep prices from equalising across borders — creating both arbitrage opportunities and policy leverage.

04 — Opportunity

A far larger opportunity set

Access to world savings, risk-sharing across countries, and returns unavailable at home — the gains that make integration worth its dangers.

The first three are frictions and risks; the fourth is the prize. Almost every policy debate in this course — convertibility, controls, pegs, bailouts — is an argument about how much of the fourth to buy with how much of the first three.

Subject matter, mapped

The field, and where this syllabus cuts it

Globalisation of trade and production

Goods crossed borders first

World trade has grown from roughly a quarter of world output in 1970 to around three-fifths today — and the composition changed even more: a rising share is intermediate goods moving inside global value chains.

Note the two visible collapses: trade falls faster than output in a crisis.

Globalisation of financial markets

Then money learned to move faster than goods

$9.6 tnTraded in currency markets on an average dayApril 2025 survey
~3 daysTime the FX market needs to turn over the value of a whole year of world merchandise trade

Most of this is not paying for imports. It is hedging, position-taking and the recycling of savings — which is why exchange rates behave like asset prices rather than like the price of a basket of goods. Hold that thought until Unit II.

Globalisation of financial markets

Countries now hold each other's balance sheets

Net positions changed slowly. Gross positions exploded — roughly fivefold relative to world output since 1980.

Why the gross number is the one that matters:

  • Exposure runs through gross claims: a country can have a small net position and still be devastated by what it owes in a currency it cannot print.
  • Exchange rate moves revalue those stocks overnight — valuation effects that no trade flow explains.
  • This is the international investment position, and reading it is a Unit II skill.
One currency is not like the others

The system runs on somebody's national money

The US dollar sits on roughly nine out of ten currency trades, prices most commodities, denominates about half of world trade invoicing and cross-border bank claims, and makes up around 58% of disclosed official reserves.

This creates what Eichengreen calls an exorbitant privilege — and, for everyone else, an exposure to another country's monetary policy.

The rupee's share is about 1.6%. That asymmetry is a political fact as much as an economic one — and the reason Unit IV exists.

A market with no closing bell

Twenty-four hours, three sessions, one market

Trading passes from Sydney and Tokyo to Singapore, Hong Kong and Mumbai, then to London — the largest single centre — and on to New York before the cycle begins again.

Two consequences worth naming now:

  • A shock anywhere is priced immediately everywhere. There is no overnight to think it over.
  • Activity concentrates in a few centres, so where a trade is booked and whose law governs it become policy questions.

Who is actually in the system

The actors and institutions

“The market” is not an abstraction. It is a short list of identifiable participants — and only some of them are trying to make money.

National authorities
  • Central banks — set policy rates, hold and manage reserves, intervene in currency markets
  • Finance ministries — issue sovereign debt and negotiate programmes
  • Regulators — decide who may hold what, and on whose balance sheet
Private participants
  • Global banks and dealers — make the FX market and move credit across borders
  • Multinational firms — invest, invoice, hedge and shift funds within the group
  • Institutional investors — pension, mutual and hedge funds; sovereign wealth funds
  • Households and migrants — remittances, at a scale that rivals aid and FDI
Multilateral and official
  • IMF — surveillance and balance of payments lending, with conditions
  • World Bank Group — development finance, guarantees, knowledge
  • BIS — the central banks' bank and its standard-setting committees
  • Regional and Islamic banks — ADB, AfDB, AIIB, NDB, IsDB
Why this is a course in international studies

Openness costs sovereignty — the choice is which kind

Pick any two. The third becomes unavailable. This is the impossible trinity, and it is the reason exchange rate policy is never merely technical.

Every position on this triangle is a statement about how much external discipline a country will accept in exchange for access to world capital — a question of sovereignty, not only of economics.

India's answer has been deliberately in the middle: a managed float, an open current account, and a capital account still opened only in parts. We return to that choice in Unit III.

Motivation, restated

Why this paper is worth your semester

As a citizen

It sets prices you did not vote on

Fuel, food oil, electronics, medicines, the cost of studying abroad and the rate on a home loan all move with the exchange rate and with global capital conditions.

As a policymaker

It defines what the state can still choose

The trilemma binds every finance ministry. Reserves, controls, swap lines and IMF access are the instruments — and each has a political price.

As a professional

It is a skill that is hired for

Treasury and risk roles, banking, trade finance, research and the multilateral institutions all begin with the material in this paper.

As a student of international studies

Finance is statecraft

Sanctions, reserve currencies, debt diplomacy, conditionality and the push for alternative institutions are exercises of power conducted through balance sheets.

If you take one idea from today: an economy's openness is a bargain — access to the world's savings, goods and markets, paid for in exposure and in policy autonomy. This course is the study of that bargain.

Wrapping up

Take away, and read before next class

  • International finance studies cross-border claims where the parties use different currencies and answer to different sovereigns.
  • Trade integration roughly doubled relative to world output since 1970; financial integration grew several times faster.
  • Gross external positions, not net ones, are where the exposure lives.
  • One national currency does most of the world's work — a privilege for its issuer and a constraint for everyone else.
  • Openness costs autonomy: the impossible trinity is the recurring choice.
