Motivation

You cannot argue about a number you cannot define

Feenstra and Taylor open the chapter with a claim worth taking seriously: the first task of any macroeconomist is to measure economic transactions. Everything else — research, forecasting, policy — sits on top of the measurement.

Consider the arguments people are actually having:

  • Is the American trade deficit a problem, or a privilege?
  • Are the surpluses of China and the oil exporters a “saving glut”?
  • Is India's current account deficit sustainable at its current size?
  • Does a government budget deficit cause an external deficit?

Not one of these can be settled — or even stated precisely — without the accounts we build today.

“To understand such debates, we must first know what is being talked about. What do all these measures mean, and how are they related?”FEENSTRA & TAYLOR, CHAPTER 5
Closed economy

One set of accounts

The national income and product accounts trace a circular flow of payments inside the border.

Open economy

Two sets of accounts

The same circular flow, plus the balance of payments accounts recording every crossing of the border.

1.3 — The national income accounts

In a closed economy, three measures are one number

Because the economy is closed, the nation can only spend what it earns and can only buy what it makes. So the three measures must coincide:

GNE = GDP = GNI

This is the benchmark to hold in your head. Every complication in the next hour comes from the same source: once the border opens, these three numbers come apart, and the differences between them are exactly the international transactions.

1.3 — The national income accounts

Three ways to count the same activity

Approach 01

Expenditure — who spent

Adds up demand for final goods and services.
Key measure: GNE

Approach 02

Product — who produced

Adds up output minus purchased inputs, that is, value added.
Key measure: GDP

Approach 03

Income — who was paid

Adds up payments to owners of labour, capital and land.
Key measures: GNI, GNDI

C — Consumption

Household spending on final goods and services: non-durables such as food, durables such as a television, and services such as a haircut.

I — Investment

Additions to the capital stock: a new house, a new factory, new equipment, and net increases in firms' inventories of unsold output.

G — Government consumption

Public purchases of goods and services — public works, defence, police, civil service. Not transfer payments such as pensions or unemployment benefit: those only move spending power around.

1.4 — Accounting for an open economy

Open the border and the three measures come apart

Rung one — from expenditure to product

GDP = GNE + TB

GDP = C + I + G + (EX − IM)EQUATION 5-1 — THE TRADE BALANCE TB = EX − IM

Some of what a country spends goes to foreign producers, so imports are subtracted. Some of what it produces is bought by foreigners, so exports are added. What is left is production inside the border.

  • TB > 0 — a trade surplus: the country sells more than it buys.
  • TB < 0 — a trade deficit. The United States in 2009: TB = −$392 billion.
Do not forget intermediates

EX and IM include parts, not just finished goods

Exported and imported intermediate goods belong here too, because in a product measure they are output and purchased inputs. Feenstra reports that as of 2010, and probably for the first time in history, world trade consisted more of intermediate goods (about 60%) than final goods (about 40%).

This is the same fact we met last week as the smile curve. It is why GDP is defined as value added, and why "the value of final goods produced" is a definition to avoid.

Rung two — from product to income

GNI = GDP + NFIA

GNI = C + I + G + (EX − IM) + (EXFS − IMFS)EQUATION 5-2 — NFIA = FACTOR SERVICE EXPORTS MINUS IMPORTS

Land, labour and capital can work in one country while being owned in another. GDP asks where output was produced. GNI asks who was paid for it.

United States, 2009: income receipts of $589 bn, income payments of $484 bn, so NFIA was +$105 bn. GDP of $14,256 bn became GNI of $14,361 bn.

Exporting factor services

Income flowing in

An Indian architect on contract in Dubai. An Indian company's profits from a subsidiary in Kenya. Interest on foreign bonds held by an Indian bank.

Importing factor services

Income flowing out

Profits of a foreign-owned car plant in Chennai, repatriated to its parent. Interest paid to foreign holders of Indian government debt.

Note the wording

"Import" and "export" describe the service, not the money

A country that hosts a great deal of foreign investment imports capital services, and pays for them. That payment leaves the country as an income outflow — the subject of the next slide.

Application — why the distinction matters

Celtic Tiger, or paper tiger?

From 1980 to 2007 Irish real GDP per person grew at a remarkable 4.1% a year. Foreign firms built the factories that did it — by some estimates 75% of Irish industrial GDP originated in foreign-owned plants.

Those owners expected to be paid. By 2000, net factor income payments abroad reached 15–20% of Irish GDP, and stayed there.

4th → 17thIreland's rank among OECD economies in 2004: fourth richest by GDP per person, only seventeenth by GNI per person

Feenstra's verdict: a country that relies on foreign investment to grow is not getting a free lunch.

Rung three — from income to disposable income

GNDI = GNI + NUT

Y = C + I + G + (EX − IM) + (EXFS − IMFS) + (UTIN − UTOUT)EQUATION 5-3 — GROSS NATIONAL DISPOSABLE INCOME

Some income crosses borders with nothing given in return: official development assistance, charitable gifts, food and medical aid in kind, and migrants' remittances to their families.

Economists prefer GNDI. GDP omits factor income; GNI omits transfers; GNDI is closest to the resources actually available to a nation's households.

223%Net unilateral transfers as a share of gross national income in Liberia, averaged over 2000–2008 — more than twice its own income

The heart of the topic

Two identities, and the second is only the first rearranged

Y = C + I + G + CAthe open-economy national income identity
Y − C − G = I + CAsubtract C and G from both sides
S ≡ Y − C − Gdefine national saving
S = I + CAthe current account identity
CA = S − Ithe form you will use most

These give two readings of the same deficit, and both are correct:

Reading one — expenditure

Spending more than you earn

CA < 0 means GNE > GNDI. The country is absorbing more goods, services and income than it produces and receives.

Reading two — saving

Saving less than you invest

CA < 0 means S < I. The country's own saving does not cover its investment, so the rest is borrowed from abroad.

This is why the press can describe the same deficit as “living beyond our means” and as “a shortage of national saving” without contradicting itself.

Breaking saving apart

Are the twin deficits really twins?

Sp = Y − T − Cprivate saving
Sg = T − Gpublic saving — the budget balance
S = Sp + Sgtaxes cancel in the aggregate
CA = Sp + Sg − Iequation 5-10

Read literally, the last line seems to say a bigger budget deficit must produce a bigger current account deficit — the famous twin deficits. Two things stop that being automatic.

Objection one

Private saving may offset it

Ricardian equivalence: if a tax cut today means a tax rise tomorrow, households save the difference, private saving rises as public saving falls, and CA does not move. Empirically the offset is real but only partial.

Objection two

Investment may move too

The large US deficits of the early 1990s came from an investment boom while total saving was rising. Government deficit falling, current account deficit rising — the opposite of twins.

0.2–0.4Percentage points of GDP by which the current account deficit moves when the government deficit moves by one point — a partial Ricardian offset, not a twin
Application

Global imbalances: someone's deficit is someone's surplus

In the industrial countries, saving and investment both fell over three decades. In the United States saving fell faster than investment, so a deficit opened; in Japan investment fell faster than saving, so a surplus opened of some 3–5% of GDP.

The mirror image sits in the developing world. Emerging markets and oil producers raised both saving and investment after the 1990s — saving by more — and moved into surplus, financing the industrial countries' deficits.

For the world as a whole, saving and investment ratios fell over the period, from near 26% of world GDP to nearer 20%. Global imbalances grew while global saving shrank.

1.4 — below the line

The other half of the ledger: trading assets

The financial account  FA = EXA − IMA
  • Records every transaction in financial and real assets between residents and non-residents — bonds, loans, equities, land, factories.
  • “Movement” means a change of ownership, not a change of location. A factory does not travel when it is sold.
  • A country that exports assets is selling claims on itself: it is borrowing.
The capital account  KA = KAIN − KAOUT
  • Small and technical for most rich countries: gifts of assets, and trade in non-produced non-financial assets such as patents, copyrights and trademarks.
  • The important case is debt forgiveness — a gift of an asset, which must be recorded so the books still balance.
  • It can matter a great deal for a poor country in a year when debt is written off.
External asset

A claim we hold on them

An Indian firm's factory in Kenya. Foreign bonds in an Indian bank's portfolio. The RBI's holdings of US Treasury bills.

External liability

A claim they hold on us

A foreign firm's plant in Chennai. Indian government bonds held abroad. Foreign portfolio holdings of Indian equities.

The result everything has been building to

CA + KA + FA = 0

CA + KA + FA = 0THE BALANCE OF PAYMENTS IDENTITY — EQUATION 5-12

We began at GNE, added everything in the balance of payments accounts, and arrived back at GNE. If adding all of it changes nothing, then all of it must sum to zero.

The reason is not economics but bookkeeping: every transaction has two sides. Every credit somewhere is a debit somewhere else.

Lender / borrowerA country with a current account surplus must be a net buyer of assets — a lender. A country in deficit must be a net seller — a borrower.

−378 + 0 + 165 = −213, not zero. The identity is exact; the measurement is not. So statisticians add an item, the statistical discrepancy, defined as whatever makes the sum come out right.

SD = −(CA + KA + FA)

Where does the error come from? Some of it is smuggling and the evasion of trade taxes. Most of it, Feenstra suggests, is financial: concealed or illicit movements of income and assets, money laundering, and capital tax evasion.

A $213 billion gap is not a rounding error. Treat the size of the discrepancy as information in its own right.

From flows to stocks

What a country owns, minus what it owes

Anne

Income $50,000

Savings of $10,000, and a credit card debt of $20,000.
Net worth: −$10,000

Beth

Income $50,000

Savings of $10,000, and no debt at all.
Net worth: +$10,000

Same income, same spending. Nobody would say they are equally well off. Countries are no different — which is why the flow accounts are not enough, and we need a stock measure too.

W = A − LEXTERNAL WEALTH = EXTERNAL ASSETS − EXTERNAL LIABILITIES
  • Also called the net international investment position, or net foreign assets. It is a stock, measured at a moment, not a flow measured over a year.
  • W > 0: a net creditor country. W < 0: a net debtor country.
  • External wealth is only part of national wealth — the rest is domestic capital, land and housing, which nobody abroad has a claim on.
−$2,866 bnUS external wealth at the end of 2009 — about −20% of GDP, or roughly $9,300 of net debt for every American, making it the largest debtor in history at the time
How external wealth moves

Thrift, the charity of others, and windfalls

ΔW = CA + KA + capital gainsEQUATION 5-16 — A COUNTRY GETS RICHER IN EXACTLY THREE WAYS
01 — Thrift

A current account surplus

Spend less than you earn; the difference buys claims on the rest of the world.

02 — The charity of others

A capital account surplus

Receive gifts of assets — most importantly, have your debts forgiven.

03 — Windfalls

Capital gains on what you already hold

Asset prices rise, or the currency moves in your favour. You did nothing, and you are richer.

The United States in 2009
External wealth, end 2008−3,654
Financial flows — net asset exports−165
Valuation: price changes+523
Valuation: exchange rate changes+277
Valuation: other+153
External wealth, end 2009−2,866

The United States borrowed a further $165 billion in 2009 — and its external wealth improved by $788 billion. Feenstra's comment: “talk about a free lunch.”

Its assets are mostly equities and foreign-currency claims, which rose as markets recovered and the dollar weakened. Its liabilities are mostly dollar debt, whose value did not move.

Capital gains are zero sum: every dollar of this windfall was a capital loss to the rest of the world.

A necessary caution

Beware of Greeks bearing statistics

We have spent two lectures learning to read national accounts. Feenstra ends the chapter by reminding us that governments produce them — and governments have interests.

Greece

A deficit that grew after the fact

Admitted to the euro in 2001 on a budget deficit reported below the 3% ceiling. In 2004 it conceded the 2003 figure was really 3.4%, and that it had not been under 3% since 1999. The full position emerged only in the crisis of 2008–09.

Italy

Il sorpasso

In 1987 Italian statisticians added about 15% to GDP as an estimate of the black economy. Italy overtook Britain overnight in the league table. No Italian felt richer.

Argentina

Reorganising the statisticians

After 2001, a government facing persistent inflation restructured its statistical bureau, which then published far lower inflation figures — conveniently reducing the cost of indexed benefits. Few believed them.

China

A price level with consequences

A revised estimate of China's price level made the country look poorer in real terms and its currency less undervalued — at a moment of intense political pressure to let the currency appreciate.

The accounts are not a photograph of the economy. They are a construction, built by people, under rules, with incentives. Learn the rules well enough to know when the numbers are being bent.

Wrapping up Unit I

What to carry into Unit II

  • In a closed economy GNE = GDP = GNI. Opening the border pulls them apart, and the gaps are the international transactions.
  • Three adjustments climb the ladder: + TB to GDP, + NFIA to GNI, + NUT to GNDI. Their sum is the current account.
  • Y = C + I + G + CA, and therefore S = I + CA. A deficit is spending above income, and saving below investment — the same fact twice.
  • CA + KA + FA = 0. Bookkeeping, not economics. It constrains everything and explains nothing.
  • Wealth is a stock: W = A − L, moved by thrift, by gifts, and by valuation effects that can dwarf both.
